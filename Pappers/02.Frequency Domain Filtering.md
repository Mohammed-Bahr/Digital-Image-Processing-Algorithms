> [!quote] **~={purple}This note introduces the second major way of processing images=~**:~={yellowlight}*in previous chapters we worked in the **~={cyan}spatial domain=~** manipulating pixels directly with masks. This chapter moves the image into the **~={cyan}frequency domain=~** using the Fourier Transform, modifies it there, and brings it back*.=~

## The core idea

**~={orange}An image can be viewed in two ways:=~**
**~={blue}Spatial domain=~** = you work directly with pixel intensities. Filtering = sliding a convolution mask over the image. (~={brown}e.g., Sobel edge detector, Gaussian blur, averaging=~) 
- ~={gray}~={pink}Intuitive=~, but expensive: for an $N \times N$ image with a $K \times K$ kernel, convolution costs roughly $O(N^2K^2)$ operations. Large kernels get very slow.=~

~={blue}**Frequency domain**=~ = you view the image as a **sum of sine/cosine waves of different frequencies**. ~={gray}Filtering becomes simple **multiplication**, which costs $O(NM \log NM)$ with the FFT nearly independent of kernel size.=~

**~={orange}So in organized way it's:=~**
	
| -                                      | Spatial domain              | Frequency domain                       |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **~={purple}Data=~**                   | Pixel intensities $f(x,y)$  | Coefficients $F(u,v)$ of sines/cosines |
| **~={purple}Filtering=~**              | **Convolution** with a mask | **Multiplication** by $H(u,v)$         |
| **~={purple}Cost for large kernels=~** | High                        | Low (using the FFT)                    |
	
~={red}**Fourier's idea=~:** *Any function that periodically repeats itself can be expressed as a ~={green}sum of sines and cosines=~ of different frequencies, each with its own coefficient*. For images, *("~={orange}frequency=~" $\rightarrow$ ~={yellow}means how fast intensity changes across space=~)*:
	
- So a complicated looking waveform is secretly just a few simple waves added together. 
- The **Fourier Transform** is the tool that finds those waves (the coefficients).
- In the **~={brown}frequency domain=~**, periodic noise appears as a few ~={green}**isolated bright spikes**=~, which can be removed surgically (notch filtering).
	
**~={orange}The mental model to internalize=~:**
	
| Frequency content                                         | Corresponds to (in the image)                                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **~={purple}Low frequencies=~** (near center of spectrum) | Slowly varying intensity $\rightarrow$ smooth regions, overall brightness, big shapes |
| **~={purple}High frequencies=~** (far from center)        | Rapid intensity changes $\rightarrow$ edges, fine details, texture, noise             |
	
The transformation is ~={brown}**lossless**=~: the inverse transform recovers the image exactly (*~={gray}up to floating-point error=~*). So you can transform, edit frequencies, and transform back.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Spatial Domain} &\rightarrow \boxed{\text{Fourier Transform}} \rightarrow
\text{Spatial Frequency Domain} 
\\[1em]
\text{Spatial Frequency Domain} 
&\rightarrow \boxed{\text{Inverse Fourier Transform}} \rightarrow 
\text{Spatial Domain}
\end{aligned}
$$
	
~={orange}**The four cases**=~: *the transform comes in four variants, depending on whether the signal is 1D or 2D and continuous or discrete*.

| Case                            | Signal                       | Used in practice for                                             | Real-world example                 |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 1. **~={blue}1D continuous=~** | $f(t)$, real-valued function | تحويل continuous signal ذات بعد واحد من spatial إلى frequency .. | Analog sound wave                  |
| 2. **~={blue}1D discrete=~**   | $f(x)$, sampled sequence     | تحويل Discrete Signal ذات بعد واحد من spatial إلى frequency ..   | Digital audio on a computer        |
| 3. **~={blue}2D continuous=~** | $f(x,y)$, analog             | تحويل اشارة مستمرة ثنائية الابعاد من spatial إلى frequency ..    | A photo before digitization (film) |
| 4. ~={blue}**2D discrete**=~   | $f(x,y)$, pixel array        | تحويل Discrete Signal ثنائية الابعاد من spatial إلى frequency .. | **A digital image**                |

## 1. The 1D DFT
	
> [!quote]+ ~={yellowlight}The one formula we need first: Euler's formula=~
> $$e^{\pm j\theta} = \cos(\theta) \pm j\sin(\theta)$$
> 
> where $j = \sqrt{-1}$. This ~={green}converts exponentials into sines/cosines=~, and gives the specific values used in hand calculations:
> $$e^{j0}=1,\quad e^{j\pi/2}=j,\quad e^{j\pi}=-1,\quad e^{j3\pi/2}=-j$$
> (*~={brown}also remember $j^2=-1$, $j^3=-j$, $j^4=1$ these appear constantly in the worked examples=~*).

### <mark style="background: #7C997CA6 ">Forward</mark> DFT

$$F(u)=\frac1M\sum_{x=0}^{M-1} f(x) \cdot \underbrace{e^{\cfrac{-2j\cdot \pi\cdot u\cdot x}{M}}}_{\text{Simple Periodic patterns}},\qquad u=0,\dots,M-1$$
Part by part:

| Symbol                                      | Meaning                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $f(x)$                                      | The ~={purple}input signal=~: ($x$ = position index)                                                             |
| $M$                                         | the number of ~={purple}samples=~                                                                                |
| $u$                                         | The ~={purple}**frequency index**=~: "how many wave cycles fit across the $M$ samples"                           |
| $e^{\cfrac{-2j\cdot \pi\cdot u\cdot x}{M}}$ | A ~={purple}**complex sinusoid**=~ completing $u$ full cycles over the $M$ samples . It's a cosine + sine pairs. |
| $\sum_x f(x)\,(\cdot)$                      | A dot product: it measures **how much of frequency $u$ exists in the signal**                                    |
| $F(u)$                                      | The output coefficient for frequency $u$. It is **complex** (real + imaginary part)                              |

So the DFT asks, for each frequency $u = 0 \dots M-1$: *"How well does a wave of this frequency match my signal?"* The answer is a complex number whose:
- **~={blue}magnitude=~** is $\rightarrow$ "~={cyan}*how much*=~" and
- **~={blue}phase=~** is $\rightarrow$ "*~={cyan}shifted by how much=~*."

### <mark style="background: #7C997CA6 ">Inverse</mark> DFT (IDFT)

$$f(x) = \sum_{u=0}^{M-1} F(u)\cdot e^{\cfrac{+2j\cdot \pi\cdot u\cdot x}{M}}, \qquad x = 0,1,\dots,M-1$$

Note the **sign flip** in the exponent. Rebuilding the signal = adding up all the frequency waves with their coefficients.

>[!note] ~={yellowlight}For ~={cyan}`DFTs`,=~ the scaling factor $1/M$ (or $1/MN$ in 2D) can go in the forward transform, the inverse, or be split between them as long as the two factors multiply to $1/M$ (or $1/MN$)=~

so Examples 4.1 and NumPy use an unscaled forward DFT with $1/M$ in the inverse while the 2D example puts $1/MN$ forward—yet all reconstruct the original exactly.


> [!example]- ~={yellowlight}Worked Example 4.1 The Core Skill :=~
>
> **Given:** $f(x) = [1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 4]$, so $M = 4$.
> 
> ### Step A Forward DFT
> 
> The exponent for each $(u, x)$ pair is $e^{-j2\pi ux/4}$. It helps to tabulate the **weights**:
> 
> | $u$ \ $x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **0** | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
> | **1** | 1 | $-j$ | $-1$ | $j$ |
> | **2** | 1 | $-1$ | 1 | $-1$ |
> | **3** | 1 | $j$ | $-1$ | $-j$ |
> 
> **$F(0)$ the DC component:** all exponents are $e^0 = 1$, so it's just the sum:
> 
> $$F(0) = 1 + 2 + 4 + 4 = 11$$
> 
> (This is the "average brightness" of the signal. With the $1/M$ convention it would be $11/4 = 2.75$.)
> 
> **$F(1)$:** $x=0,1,2,3$ gives angles $0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}$, i.e. weights $1, -j, -1, j$:
> 
> $$F(1) = 1(1) + 2(-j) + 4(-1) + 4(j) = 1 - 2j - 4 + 4j = -3 + 2j$$
> 
> **$F(2)$:** weights $1, -1, 1, -1$ (angles $0,\pi,2\pi,3\pi$):
> 
> $$F(2) = 1 + 2(-1) + 4(1) + 4(-1) = -1$$
> 
> **$F(3)$:** weights $1, j, -1, -j$:
> 
> $$F(3) = 1 + 2(j) + 4(-1) + 4(-j) = -3 - 2j$$
> 
> **Final spectrum:**
> 
> $$F(0)=11,\quad F(1)=-3+2j,\quad F(2)=-1,\quad F(3)=-3-2j$$
> 
> **Two insights worth remembering:**
> 1. $F(3) = \overline{F(1)}$ (complex conjugate). For any **real** input, the spectrum is conjugate-symmetric: $F(M-u) = \overline{F(u)}$. Half the spectrum is redundant this always happens with images.
> 2. Magnitudes are $|F(0)|=11$, $|F(1)|=|F(3)|=\sqrt{3^2+2^2}=\sqrt{13}\approx3.6$, $|F(2)|=1$. Most energy sits at low frequency the signal $[1,2,4,4]$ is fairly smooth, so that makes sense.
> 
> ### Step B Inverse DFT (prove we get the original back)
> 
> Now $f(x) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_u F(u)\,e^{+j2\pi ux/4}$. The weight tables (positive exponent now):
> 
> | $x$ \ $u$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **0** | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
> | **1** | 1 | $j$ | $-1$ | $-j$ |
> | **2** | 1 | $-1$ | 1 | $-1$ |
> | **3** | 1 | $-j$ | $-1$ | $j$ |
> 
> **$f(0)$** (weights all 1 the imaginary parts cancel):
> 
> $$f(0)=\tfrac14\big[11+(-3+2j)+(-1)+(-3-2j)\big]=\tfrac14(4)=1 \checkmark$$
> 
> **$f(1)$** (weights $1, j, -1, -j$). Expand the complex products using $j^2=-1$:
> - $(-3+2j)(j) = -3j + 2j^2 = -2-3j$
> - $(-3-2j)(-j) = 3j + 2j^2 = -2+3j$
> 
> $$f(1)=\tfrac14\big[11+(-2-3j)+1+(-2+3j)\big]=\tfrac14(8)=2 \checkmark$$
> 
> **$f(2)$** (weights $1,-1,1,-1$):
> 
> $$f(2)=\tfrac14\big[11-(-3+2j)+(-1)-(-3-2j)\big]=\tfrac14\big[11+3-2j-1+3+2j\big]=\tfrac14(16)=4 \checkmark$$
> 
> **$f(3)$** (weights $1,-j,-1,j$):
> - $(-3+2j)(-j)=3j-2j^2 = 2+3j$
> - $(-3-2j)(j)=-3j-2j^2 = 2-3j$
> 
> $$f(3)=\tfrac14\big[11+(2+3j)+1+(2-3j)\big]=\tfrac14(16)=4 \checkmark$$
> 
> We recover exactly $[1, 2, 4, 4]$ the lossless property, demonstrated numerically. **Notice the pattern:** in every step, the imaginary parts cancel in conjugate pairs, leaving a real result. That's what conjugate symmetry guarantees.

## 2. The 2D DFT

$$F(u,v) = \sum_{x=0}^{M-1}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1} f(x,y) 
\cdot
e^{ \displaystyle -2j\pi \cdot (\cfrac{ux}{M} + \cfrac{vy}{N} )}
\qquad 
,\text{2D DFT}
$$

$$f(x,y) = \frac{1}{MN}\sum_{u=0}^{M-1}\sum_{v=0}^{N-1} F(u,v) \cdot
e^{ \displaystyle +2j\pi \cdot (\cfrac{ux}{M} + \cfrac{vy}{N} )}
\qquad 
,\text{Inverse 2D DFT}
$$

(~={gray}*Again, the lecture's example puts $\frac1{MN}$ on the forward side instead*=~.)
- $M \times N$ = ~={blue}image size=~, $(x,y)$ ~={blue}pixel position=~, $(u,v)$ = a **2D frequency** (*~={yellow}$u$ = horizontal frequency, $v$ = vertical frequency=~*).
- The double sum is just the 1D idea applied in two directions rows and then columns (*~={gray}in practice: DFT of every row, then DFT of every column of the result=~*).
- Every coefficient $F(u,v)$ is a ~={brown}**complex number**=~ (~={gray}real part + imaginary part=~).
	
>[!quote] ~={yellowlight}$F(0,0)$ is the DC component. With the convention used here, $F(0,0)$ is the sum of all pixel intensities, and $F(0,0)/(MN)$ is the image mean. If $1/(MN)$ is placed in the forward DFT instead, then $F(0,0)$ directly equals the mean=~
	
>[!quote] ~={red}$f(x,y)$ tells us the intensity at a spatial location  $F(u,v)$ tells us how strongly a particular 2D spatial-frequency pattern exists in the image.=~
	
> [!example]- ~={yellowlight}Worked 2D example=~
> 
> Image ($M=3$ columns, $N=2$ rows):
> 
> | | $x=0$ | $x=1$ | $x=2$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $y=0$ | 5 | 10 | 20 |
> | $y=1$ | 5 | 15 | 5 |
> 
> **Compute $F(0,0)$:** with $u=v=0$ every exponent is $e^0=1$, so it's the average of all pixels:
> 
> $$F(0,0)=\frac{1}{MN}\sum_{x,y} f(x,y)=\frac{1}{6}(5+10+20+5+15+5)=\frac{60}{6}=10$$
> 
> **Key fact (stated on the slide): $F(0,0)$ = the average intensity of the image** the DC component. Zero out $F(0,0)$ and the whole image darkens to mean zero  scale it and you change overall brightness.
> 
> **Compute $F(0,1)$:** now $u=0, v=1$. The exponent becomes $e^{-j2\pi y/2}=e^{-j\pi y}$, which is $1$ for $y=0$ and $-1$ for $y=1$ it **depends only on $y$**. So each column contributes (top row − bottom row):
> 
> - $x=0$: $5-5=0$
> - $x=1$: $10-15=-5$
> - $x=2$: $20-5=15$
> 
> $$S = 0+(-5)+15=10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad F(0,1)=\frac{10}{6}=\frac{5}{3}\approx 1.667$$
> 
> **Interpretation:** $F(0,1)$ measures *vertical* variation (difference between rows). The same recipe is repeated for the remaining $(u,v)$ pairs to fill the whole spectrum in general, $M \times N$ coefficients for an $M \times N$ image.

## Filtering: the pipeline
$$g(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}{H(u,v),F(u,v)}$$
$$
f(x,y)\ \xrightarrow{\text{pre-process}}\ \boxed{\text{DFT}}\ \rightarrow\ F(u,v)\ \rightarrow\ \boxed{\times H(u,v)}\ \rightarrow\ G(u,v)\ \xrightarrow{\text{Inverse DFT}}\ \text{post-process}\ \rightarrow\ g(x,y)
$$
1. ~={purple}**Compute the DFT**=~ $F(u,v)$ of the image.
2. ~={purple}**Multiply**=~ by a filter function $H(u,v)$ (*~={gray}a same-sized array of values, usually real, between 0 and 1=~*).
3. ~={purple}**Compute the inverse DFT**=~ of $H(u,v)F(u,v)$ to get the enhanced image $g(x,y)$.
	
This works because of the ~={red}**convolution theorem**=~:$$f(x,y) * h(x,y) \ \Longleftrightarrow\  F(u,v)\,H(u,v)$$
> Convolution ($*$) in the **spatial domain** = point-by-point **multiplication** in the **frequency domain**.
	
instead of an expensive convolution with a big mask, you multiply two arrays of the same size. Filtering can be done in **either** domain the results are equivalent but the cost and the intuition differ.

## The Low&High pass filters

- $D(u,v)$ = ~={blue}**Euclidean distance=~ from the center of the spectrum** (~={gray}*after centering, the center is where the DC term $F(0,0)$ sits*=~):

$$D(u,v) = \sqrt{(u - P/2)^2 + (v - Q/2)^2}$$

  for a $P\times Q$ (padded) image. Points near the center = low frequencies  points near the border = high frequencies.
- $D_0$ = **~={pink}cutoff frequency=~** a radius (in pixels) you choose. It's the "knob" controlling how aggressive the filter is.

### Lowpass filters (smoothing/blurring)

After the DFT, your image is represented in the **frequency domain**:
The **~={blue}center=~** represents low frequencies slowly changing regions:
- smooth background
- overall brightness
	
The ~={blue}**outside**=~ represents high frequencies:
- edges
- fine details
- noise
- sudden changes
	
~={red}So a low-pass filter says:=~
>***Keep the center, reduce/remove the outside*.**
	
so **~={brown}A low-pass filter attenuates high frequencies=~.** That's why the output becomes ~={green}**smoother/blurry**=~.
	
#### Lowpass Filters

> [!quote]+ ~={yellowlight}What is $H(u,v)$?=~
> 
> The filter is basically a ~={green}**mask**=~: $H(u,v)$
> It tells us:
> > "*~={yellow}How much of this frequency should I keep?=~*"
> 
> Then we multiply: $$\boxed{G(u,v)=H(u,v)F(u,v)}$$
> 
> where:
> - $F(u,v)$ = original frequency spectrum
> - $H(u,v)$ = filter
> - $G(u,v)$ = filtered spectrum
	
>[!note] ~={yellowlight}$D(u,v)$ is the Euclidean distance represent the distance from a point in the frequency domain plane to the origin and the designated cutoff frequency radius, $D_0$ The positive **cutoff frequency** radius, $n$ The **order** of the filter=~
	
![](https://i-blog.csdnimg.cn/direct/9f576724878c43b190ca14c1fde1d632.png)

| Filter                               | $H(u,v)$                                                                                             | Behavior                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **~={purple}Ideal=~ (`ILPF`)**       | $$H(u,v) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } D(u,v) \leq D_0 \\ 0 & \text{if } D(u,v) > D_0 \end{cases}$$ | Hard cut-off ("very sharp"). Causes **ringing** artifacts.                                             |
| **~={purple}Butterworth=~ (`BLPF`)** | $$H(u,v) = \frac{1}{1 + [D(u,v)/D_0]^{2n}}$$                                                         | Smooth roll-off. Order $n$ controls steepness: large $n$ approaches ideal. Little ringing for low $n$. |
| **~={purple}Gaussian=~ (`GLPF`)**    | $$H(u,v) = e^{\cfrac{-D^2(u,v)}{2D_0^2}}$$                                                           | Smoothest ("very smooth"), **no ringing**. $D_0$ acts as $\sigma$.                                     |

~={orange}**How to read each formula:**=~

- ~={red}**Ideal=~ (*~={yellowlight}ON/OFF switch=~*):** Either keep it or completely remove it.
- ~={red}**Butterworth=~ (*~={yellowlight}Adjustable Dimmer=~*):** Smooth, but you can control how sharp the transition is with $n$.
- ~={red}**Gaussian=~ = ~={yellowlight}(*natural fading*)=~ :** Very smooth, no abrupt transition $\rightarrow$ no noticeable ringing
	
### Highpass filters (sharpening)

**Idea:** edges and fine details are high frequencies, so to sharpen you _==🟡attenuate the low frequencies_ and keep the high ones==_.$$G(u,v)=H_{hp}(u,v)\cdot F(u,v)$$
Every highpass filter is the complement of its lowpass partner: $$H_{hp}(u,v)=1-H_{lp}(u,v)$$

| Filter                               | $H(u,v)$                                                                                             | Character                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **~={purple}Ideal=~ (`IHPF`)**       | $$H(u,v) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } D(u,v) \leq D_0 \\ 1 & \text{if } D(u,v) > D_0 \end{cases}$$ | Hard cut. Causes **ringing**.                    |
| **~={purple}Butterworth=~ (`BHPF`)** | $$H(u,v) = \cfrac{1}{1 + [\cfrac{D_0}{D(u,v)}]^{2n}}$$                                               | Smooth transition. Order $n$ controls steepness. |
| **~={purple}Gaussian=~ (`GHPF`)**    | $$H(u,v) = 1 - e^{\cfrac{-D^2(u,v)}{2D_0^2}}$$                                                       | Smoothest, **no ringing**.                       |

![563](https://velog.velcdn.com/images/milkbottle0305/post/f20f01fc-4f78-49f6-b311-a61afa4fbfbe/image.png)
**~={orange}Reading the shapes=~:**
- The 3-D plots look like a lowpass filter flipped upside down: a hole in the middle and a flat plateau at 1 elsewhere.
- `IHPF` has a vertical wall. `BHPF` and `GHPF` rise gradually.
- The `BHPF` formula has $D_0/D$ (inverted) compared to the lowpass $D/D_0$. At $D=D_0$ it equals $0.5$ for any $n$.

~={orange}**What the results look like**=~: the output is mostly **black with ~={pink}bright outlines**=~. Smooth areas are removed, and only edges remain. Ideal gives ringing around the edges. Butterworth and Gaussian are cleaner.
	
~={orange}**Why it goes dark=~:** $F(0,0)$ is the average brightness, and it sits at the very center, which every highpass filter zeroes (or nearly). With the DC term gone, the mean of the output is about 0, so smooth regions become black.

## Bandpass filter

**Idea:** keep only a ~={brown}**middle band**=~ of frequencies. Reject very low _and_ very high ones.
	![](https://cdn.everythingrf.com/files/1572866567213_637084633678839181_637958888678537148.png)
- ~={purple}Frequencies below=~ $F_L$ are attenuated (~={gray}lower cutoff=~).
- ~={purple}Frequencies above=~ $F_H$ are attenuated (~={gray}upper cutoff=~).
- ~={purple}Frequencies between=~ $F_L$ and $F_H$ pass.
	
~={orange}**Why use it=~:** suppressing low frequencies enhances edges, and suppressing high frequencies reduces noise. A bandpass does both at once.
	
~={orange}**Construction=~:** a bandpass is a combination of a lowpass and a highpass. For example, with ideal filters: $$H_{bp}(u,v)=\begin{cases}1 & D_0\le D(u,v)\le D_1\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
Equivalently, $H_{bp}=H_{lp}(D_1)\cdot H_{hp}(D_0)$. In the lecture figure (the trapezoid curve in dB), the cutoffs are where the response drops about 3 dB below the passband, and the filter order sets how steep the sides are (dB/octave). Smooth versions use Butterworth or Gaussian pieces instead of ideal ones.
	
The **complement**, a bandreject (notch) filter, removes a band instead, and is what you'd use for periodic noise.

## Homomorphic filtering

>[!error] ~={red}An image often has uneven lighting: some regions are in shadow and others are overexposed, so details hide in the dark parts.**=~
>- **Uneven illumination** → الإضاءة غير متساوية.
>- **Poor contrast** → التفاصيل في المناطق المظلمة مش واضحة.
	
~={blue}**Goal**=~: ~={green}**compress or reduce the illumination range and enhance contrast at the same time**=~.
### The illumination-reflectance model

$$f(x,y)=i(x,y)\cdot r(x,y)$$
- $i(x,y)$ = ~={purple}**illumination**=~, what the light source provides. It varies **slowly** across space (~={brown}*low frequency*=~).
- $r(x,y)$ = ~={purple}**reflectance**=~, how the objects reflect light. It varies **abruptly** ~={gray}مفاجئ=~, especially at junctions ~={gray}نقاط التقاء=~ between different objects (*~={brown}high frequency=~*).
	
### Why we can't filter directly

The Fourier transform of a _product_ is not the product of transforms: $$\mathcal{F}[i \cdot r]\neq \mathcal{F}[i]\cdot\mathcal{F}[r]$$So we can't separate the two components in the frequency domain. But the transform _is_ linear over **sums**, ~={green}so we convert the product into a sum with a logarithm=~.
لان Fourier Transform يتعامل بشكل ممتاز مع **الجمع**.
$$
\mathcal{F}[i + r] = \mathcal{F}[i] +\mathcal{F}[r]
$$
### Derivation, step by step
**~={orange}The pipeline:=~**$$\boxed{f(x,y) \to \ln \to \text{DFT} \to H(u,v) \to \text{DFT}^{-1} \to \exp \to g(x,y)}$$**1. ~={purple}Take the log=~:** *~={brown}(From $\ln(ab) = \ln(a) +\ln(b)$)=~*
$$z(x,y)=\ln (f(x,y))=\ln (i(x,y))+\ln (r(x,y))$$
**2. ~={purple}Transform=~:** $$Z(u,v)=F_i(u,v)+F_r(u,v)$$where $F_i=\mathcal{F}[\ln i]$ and $F_r=\mathcal{F}[\ln r]$.
**3. ~={purple}Apply a filter=~ $H(u,v)$:**$$S(u,v)=H(u,v)Z(u,v)=F_iH+F_rH$$**4. ~={purple}Inverse transform=~:** $$s(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[F_iH]+\mathcal{F}^{-1}[F_rH]=i'(x,y)+r'(x,y)$$**5. ~={purple}Undo the log with exp=~:**$$g(x,y)=e^{s(x,y)}=e^{i'}\cdot e^{r'}=i_0(x,y)\cdot r_0(x,y)$$

### The filter

The filter is ~={green}**highpass in character**=~: it ~={yellow}suppresses low-frequency illumination=~ and ~={yellow}boosts high-frequency reflectance=~. *(~={gray}The lecture uses a generalized Gaussian=~)*:$$H(u,v)=(\gamma_H-\gamma_L)\left(1-e^{-D^2(u,v)/2D_0^2}\right)+\gamma_L$$**~={purple}What each part does=~:**
- $1-e^{\cfrac{-D^2}{2D_0^2}}$ is the Gaussian highpass, going from 0 at the center up to 1 far away.
- At $D=0$ (*~={brown}lowest frequencies=~*): $H=\gamma_L$.
- At $D\to\infty$ (*~={brown}highest frequencies=~*): $H=\gamma_H$.
- So $\gamma_L$ is the **gain on illumination** and $\gamma_H$ is the **gain on reflectance**.
	
**~={purple}Choosing the parameters=~:**
- $\gamma_L<1$ compresses the dynamic range (dims bright illumination, lifts shadows).
- $\gamma_H>1$ boosts contrast and detail.
- Typical values are $\gamma_L\approx0.25$–$0.5$ and $\gamma_H\approx1.5$–$2$.
- $D_0$ sets where the transition between "illumination" and "reflectance" happens.

This curve shows exactly this: it starts at $\gamma_L$ at the origin and rises smoothly to a plateau at $\gamma_H$.
