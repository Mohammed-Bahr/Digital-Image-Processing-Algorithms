## Part 1: Lecture 1, Foundations

### 1.1 What is image processing?

>**~={red}Image processing=~** is a method of performing operations on an image to **enhance it** or **extract useful information** from it. 
	
It's a type of *signal processing* where the **input is an image**. The **output is either**:
- **~={yellow}another image=~** (enhanced, denoised, sharpened), or
- ~={yellow}**features or parameters**=~ describing the image (edges, objects, labels).
	
An image is treated as a **2D signal**, so classic signal-processing tools (filters, Fourier transforms) apply to it. DIP is about building algorithms that do this on a computer.

### 1.2 Image Processing vs. Computer Vision

This is the single most-confused distinction, so let me lock it down.

|                                             | Image Processing (IP/DIP)             | Computer Vision (CV)                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| ~={orange}**Input**=~                       | An image                              | An image (often a processed one)          |
| ~={orange}**Output**=~                      | Another image **or** numbers/features | A **decision / action / understanding**   |
| ~={orange}**Goal**=~                        | Enhance, restore, transform           | See, recognize, interpret                 |
| ~={orange}**Examples**=~                    | Denoising, sharpening, contrast       | Face recognition, autonomous driving, OCR |
| ~={orange}**Where it sits in a pipeline**=~ | Early / middle stages                 | Later stages                              |
	
>[!Quote] *AI enables computers to think. Computer vision enables them to see, observe, and understand.*
>--- from the lecture
	
**Practical pipeline view:**
$$
\text{Camera} \rightarrow Image Acquisition \rightarrow Enhancement \rightarrow Restoration \rightarrow Segmentation
\rightarrow Feature Extraction \rightarrow Classification/Recognition (CV) \rightarrow Decision
$$

~={red}**The CV task spectrum**=~ (*~={gray}illustrated with the cat-and-ball image know the differences=~*):
	![](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335013237/figure/fig1/AS:789103180271616@1565148267602/The-differences-among-six-popular-computer-vision-tasks-1-Object-recognition-sometimes.jpg)
1. ~={purple}**Object recognition=~ (~={yellow}classification=~):** whole image $\rightarrow$ one label ("Cat")
2. ~={purple}**Object detection=~:** find *where* each object is $\rightarrow$ bounding boxes ("cat" box, "ball" box)
3. ~={purple}**Semantic segmentation=~:** label **every pixel** (red = cat, green = ball, blue = background)
4. ~={purple}**Image captioning=~:** image $\rightarrow$ text description ("A cat is playing a ball")
5. **~={purple}Visual question answering=~:** image + question ("How many balls?") $\rightarrow$ answer ("One")
6. ~={purple}**Image generation=~:** text $\rightarrow$ image

These go from *low-level pixel work* (image processing) up to *high-level understanding* (CV/AI).
	
## 1.3 Key stages of a DIP pipeline

$$
\begin{array}{c c c c}
\boxed{\text{Image Enhancement}} & \xrightarrow{} & \boxed{\text{Image Restoration}} & \xrightarrow{} & \boxed{\text{Morphological Processing}} \\
\uparrow & & \uparrow & & \downarrow \\
\boxed{\text{Image Acquisition}} & & \boxed{\text{Colour Image Processing}} & \xrightarrow{\text{dashed}} & \boxed{\text{Image Compression}} & \xrightarrow{\text{dashed}} & \text{...} \\
\text{Problem Domain} & & & & \downarrow \\
& & & & \boxed{\text{Segmentation}} \\
& & & & \downarrow \\
& & & & \boxed{\text{Object Recognition}} \\
& & & & \downarrow \\
& & & & \boxed{\text{Representation \& Description}}
\end{array}
$$

1. ~={blue}**Image Acquisition**=~ *(~={gray}الحصول على الصور=~)*: retrieve an image from a source (camera, sensor, scanner). The ~={cyan}**first and most crucial**=~ step: no image → nothing to process.
2. ~={blue}**Image Enhancement**=~ improve quality / bring out hidden information (e.g., brighten a dark photo). It's *subjective* "better" depends on the goal.
3. ~={blue}**Image Restoration**=~ (*~={gray}استعادة الصور=~*) recover an image from a **degraded** version (blurred, noisy, damaged old documents). It's more *objective* you try to undo a known degradation.
4. ~={blue}**Morphological Processing**=~ (*~={gray}المعالجة الصرفية=~*) deals with **~={cyan}shapes and structures=~**: pixels are added or removed so objects' forms can be cleaned up and identified (e.g., removing specks, filling holes after segmentation).
5. **~={blue}Image Segmentation=~** divide the image into segments/regions to **locate objects and boundaries**. Goal: ~={brown}simplify the representation into something more meaningful and easier to analyze=~ (e.g., dog photos → dog silhouettes).
6. **~={blue}Image Classification / Recognition=~** identify what's in the image: a model outputs probabilities ("Dog 94%, Wolf 4%, Cat 2%"). This is where deep learning/ML plugs in.
7. ~={blue}**Representation & Description**=~ convert segmented regions into features a computer can reason about.
	
>[!note] ~={White}**Enhancement vs. restoration=~:** enhancement is subjective ("looks better"), while restoration is objective ("undo a known degradation").

# Part 2: Lecture 2, Representation and Enhancement

## 2.1 How a digital image is represented

A digital image is a ~={green}**matrix of pixels**=~. Each **pixel** holds an **intensity value** (brightness number). **Resolution** = number of pixels, e.g., a 6×6 image has 36 pixels.

- ~={red}**Grayscale image=~:** is a one matrix $f(x,y)$, each ~={orange}pixel=~ = 1 and  ~={orange}intensity value=~ is between 0 = black … 255 = white for 8-bit.
	![](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Antonio-Neves/publication/325569674/figure/fig3/AS:779763010506755@1562921397951/Matrix-for-certain-area-of-a-grayscale-image-17.jpg)
	- ~={purple}The number of gray levels=~ is $L = 2^k$ for $k$ bits per pixel ($k=8 \Rightarrow L=256$).
	- ~={purple}**Resolution**=~ $=$ rows × columns, and total pixels = rows × columns (a 6×6 image has 36 pixels).
- ~={red}**Color (RGB) image=~:** **three matrices (~={orange}channels=~)** stacked Red, Green, Blue. 
	- Each pixel is a triple `(R, G, B)`. Splitting a color image yields 3 independent intensity planes.
	
~={blue}**Coordinate convention=~:** the origin is at the **top-left**, $x$ goes right and $y$ goes down. In code, indexing is `img[row, col] = img[y, x]`. This confuses many people.

```python
import cv2, numpy as np

img = cv2.imread("photo.jpg")          # BGR order in OpenCV, not RGB!
print(img.shape, img.dtype)            # (H, W, 3) uint8
b, g, r = cv2.split(img)               # three channels
gray = cv2.imread("photo.jpg", cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE)  # (H, W)
```

## 2.2 RGB to grayscale

Two standard methods (applied **pixel-by-pixel**):
$$I_{gray} = \frac{R+G+B}{3}\quad \text{(Average method)}$$
$$I_{gray} = 0.3R + 0.59G + 0.11B\quad\text{(weighted method)}$$

The weighted method matches human perception, which is most sensitive to green and least to blue. 
so this approximates perceived *luminance* (the rigorous standard weights are 0.299/0.587/0.114 the slide uses the rounded 0.3/0.59/0.11)..

## 2.3 Image enhancement

**~={red}Goal=~:** make an image more useful, either visually appealing, with specific features brought out, or less noisy. It simplifies later extraction and decision-making in machine-vision systems.

~={red}**Two families of Enhancements methods:**=~

| Domain                    | Idea                                                                                                                          | Tools                                                          |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ~={orange}**Spatial**=~   | Manipulate pixels **directly** in image coordinates (~={gray}origin top-left; x $\rightarrow$ right, y $\rightarrow$ down=~). | Point processing, histogram methods, spatial filters           |
| ~={orange}**Frequency**=~ | Transform with the **Fourier transform**, modify frequency components, transform back                                         | Low-pass, high-pass filters (the "filters" box in the diagram) |
	
**~={red}Three types of operations (~={gray}by how much input each output pixel depends on=~)=~**.

| Type                   | Output at $(x,y)$ depends on    | Example                                   |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| ~={purple}**Point**=~  | Only the input pixel at $(x,y)$ | Negative, gamma, thresholding             |
| ~={purple}**Local**=~  | A neighborhood around $(x,y)$   | Blur, sharpen, median filter              |
| ~={purple}**Global**=~ | All pixels in the image         | Histogram equalization, Fourier transform |
	
~={red}**The master equation of spatial domain enhancement:**=~$$g(x,y) = T[f(x,y)]$$$f(x,y)$ is the ~={green}input image=~ (~={gray}*pixel at position x,y*=~), $g(x, y)$ the ~={green}output=~ processed image, and $T$ an ~={green}operator defined over some neighborhood=~ of $(x,y)$. 
When the neighborhood is **just the pixel itself** ,$T$ acts on a single number so this simplifies to: $$s = T(r)$$
$r \rightarrow$  is the ~={green}input intensity=~ and $s \rightarrow$ the ~={green}output intensity=~. 
Point operations are therefore **intensity mapping functions** (curves), which is why every one below is plotted as a curve.

## 2.4 Point processing techniques

Let $L$ = number of gray levels (256), so intensities run $0 \ldots L-1$.

### (1) Negative image

$$s = (L-1) - r$$

- Flips dark and light so it basically ~={pink}Inverts brightness=~
- It helps when interesting detail is white or gray on a dominant dark background (X-rays, medical images).
```python
neg = 255 - gray              # NumPy broadcasting; uint8 safe here
```

### (2) Logarithmic transformation

$$s = c \cdot\log(1+r)$$

- `r` = input pixel, `s` = output pixel
- `c` = scaling constant (usually chosen so the max input maps to max output)
- `1 + r` avoids `log(0)` (r can be 0)
	
The curve rises steeply for low $r$ and flattens for high $r$. It ~={pink}**expands dark values**=~ into a wide output range and ~={pink}**compresses bright values**=~.
	
**~={purple}Use case=~:** dark images (the building photo on the slide becomes visibly brighter), and displaying Fourier spectra whose values span an enormous range.
	
~={brown}Choose $c$ so the output fits the range=~: $c = \cfrac{255}{\log(1+r_{max})}$.

```python
c = 255 / np.log(1 + gray.max())
log_img = (c * np.log1p(gray.astype(np.float64))).astype(np.uint8)
```

### (3) Power-law (gamma) transformation

$$s = c \cdot r^{\gamma}$$
- `c` is generally set to 1
- `r` must be **~={orange}normalized to the range $[0,1]$** =~first (divide the pixel by 255), ~={cyan}then scale the result back to $[0,255]$=~,
	![](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/322938595/figure/fig2/AS:779405756493847@1562836221719/Power-law-transformations-curves-generated-with-different-r-values.gif)

| $\gamma$     | Effect                                                                   | Use                                                                      | details                                                                                                                      |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $\gamma < 1$ | ~={purple}Brightens=~: expands dark values, compresses bright (like log) | Dark images (the spine MRI at 0.6, 0.4, 0.3 gets progressively brighter) | curve above the identity line $\rightarrow$ **~={green}expands dark values=~ $\rightarrow$ ~={cyan}brightens dark images=~** |
| $\gamma = 1$ | ~={purple}Identity=~                                                     | No change                                                                |                                                                                                                              |
| $\gamma > 1$ | ~={purple}Darkens=~: compresses dark values, expands bright              | Washed-out, overexposed images                                           | darkens over-exposed/washed-out images<br>                                                                                   |
![[Foundations&Enhancement of DIP-1791106397069.webp]]
	
```python
def gamma_correct(img, gamma, c=1.0):
    r = img / 255.0
    s = c * np.power(r, gamma)
    return np.clip(s * 255, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8)

# fast version using a lookup table (what OpenCV pipelines usually do)
lut = (255 * (np.arange(256)/255.0) ** 0.5).astype(np.uint8)
out = cv2.LUT(gray, lut)
```
	
>[!note] Because point operations depend only on $r$, you can precompute all 256 outputs in a **lookup table (LUT)** and apply it in one pass, which is very fast.
	
### (4) Piecewise-linear transformation (contrast stretching with control points)

Defined by two control points $(r_1,s_1)$ and $(r_2,s_2)$, giving three linear segments:

$$  
s=\begin{cases}  
\dfrac{s_1}{r_1} \cdot r & 0\le r\le r_1 \\   
\dfrac{s_2-s_1}{r_2-r_1}(r-r_1)+s_1 & r_1<r\le r_2 \\   
\dfrac{(L-1)-s_2}{(L-1)-r_2}(r-r_2)+s_2 & r_2<r\le L-1  
\end{cases}  
$$

Each piece is $\text{slope}\times(r-\text{start})+\text{start output}$. A steep middle segment (slope > 1) **spreads out** the mid-range intensities, which boosts contrast there. The slide example is a low-contrast pollen image made vivid this way.

```python
def piecewise(img, r1, s1, r2, s2, L=256):
    r = img.astype(np.float64)
    out = np.where(r <= r1, (s1/r1)*r,
          np.where(r <= r2, ((s2-s1)/(r2-r1))*(r-r1) + s1,
                            ((L-1-s2)/(L-1-r2))*(r-r2) + s2))
    return out.astype(np.uint8)
```

Watch for division by zero if $r_1 = 0$ or $r_2 = L-1$.

## 2.5 Histogram-based methods

الـ Histogram Methods كلها مبنية على فكرة واحدة:
> **نبص على توزيع الـ pixel intensities ونستخدمه لتحسين الـ contrast**
### Image histogram
an **image histogram** is a graphical representation of the **number of pixels at each intensity value**. 
Formally, for each gray level `rₖ`:
$$h(r_k) = n_k$$
- $n_k\rightarrow$ is the ~={yellow}number of pixels with intensity value=~ $r_k$.
- Each **bin** covers an intensity range, and the bin's value is the pixel count.
- يعني الـ bin هو ببساطة **مكان في الـ histogram يمثل intensity أو range من intensities**.

**~={red}Normalized histogram=~** (~={brown}**PDF**, the probability of intensity $r_k$):=~

$$p(r_k) = \frac{n_k}{n}, \quad n = \text{total pixels}$$
- ال $n_k$​ = عدد pixels عند intensity $r_k$
- ال $n$ = إجمالي عدد الـ pixels
**How to read one:**
	![](https://irp.cdn-website.com/13e20eff/dms3rep/multi/opt/Picture1-52da0197-640w.jpg)
- Concentrated on the ~={purple}**left**=~ $\rightarrow$ ~={green}dark image=~.
- Concentrated on the **~={purple}right=~** $\rightarrow$ ~={green}bright image=~.
- ~={purple}**Narrow**=~ $\rightarrow$ ~={green}low contrast=~.
- ~={purple}**Wide=~ and spread out** $\rightarrow$ ~={green}high contrast=~.
	
```python
hist = np.bincount(gray.ravel(), minlength=256)     # manual
hist = cv2.calcHist([gray], [0], None, [256], [0,256]).ravel()  # OpenCV
```

For RGB images, you compute a histogram per channel..
	
### Contrast stretching (min-max normalization)

Linearly maps $[X_{min}, X_{max}]$ onto the full $[0,255]$. The histogram shape stays the same and is just ~={orange}**stretched**=~ wider:
$$X_{new} = \frac{X_{input}-X_{min}}{X_{max}-X_{min}}\times 255$$
- $X_{input}$ = is the pixel value and $X_{min}$, $X_{max}$ is min/max **of the image**.
- $X_{min} \rightarrow 0$  and $X_{max} \rightarrow 255$ exactly, ~={cyan}and everything else maps proportionally in between these two values=~
- This is a linear remap a special case of piecewise linear where the control points are $(X_{min}, 0)$ and $(X_{max}, 255)$
	
> [!example]- ~={yellowlight}**Worked example=~:** 
> $X_{min}=50$, $X_{max}=180$
> 
> |Input|Calculation|Output|
> |---|---|---|
> |50|$\frac{0}{130}\cdot255$|**0**|
> |60|$\frac{10}{130}\cdot255 = 19.6$|**20**|
> |100|$\frac{50}{130}\cdot255 = 98.08$|**98**|
> |180|$\frac{130}{130}\cdot255$|**255**|
> 

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:720/format:webp/1*eb6sN9rP4eA3ttbVzpFX6Q.png)$$
\text{Input Image before Contrast Stretching with its histogram}
$$![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:720/format:webp/1*wH52JLSYwDim0T44OBlhyw.png)$$
\text{Input Image after Contrast Stretching with its histogram}
$$
	
```python
def stretch(img):
    mn, mx = img.min(), img.max()
    return ((img - mn) / (mx - mn) * 255).astype(np.uint8)
# OpenCV: cv2.normalize(img, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX)
```
	
> [!warning] **Weakness:**~={yellowlight} a single outlier pixel (one very dark or very bright) ruins the range. Real implementations clip at percentiles (e.g., 2nd and 98th) instead of real min/max values.=~

### Histogram equalization

~={purple}**Goal=~:** ~={yellowlight}remap intensities so the output histogram is (approximately) **uniform** every gray level used equally $\rightarrow$ maximum dynamic range $\rightarrow$ maximum contrast.=~
	
using the image's own ~={red}**cumulative distribution function (CDF)**=~ (*~={gray}دالة التوزيع التراكمي=~*) as the mapping curve. 
So :
	![](https://encrypted-tbn0.gstatic.com/images?q=tbn:ANd9GcSnGLMXcmSrPKDGp3IkrTrw3Dz4rStnFjwC08oKqAxYTQ&s=10)
1. ~={blue}intensity Dense=~ ranges get ~={cyan}spread out=~ more and 
2. ~={blue}sparse Denses=~ ones get ~={cyan}compressed=~.
	
~={orange}**Algorithm:**=~
1. ~={purple}**Compute**=~ the histogram $n_k$ for each gray level.
2. ~={red}**PDF**=~: $p_k = \cfrac{n_k}{n}$
3. **~={red}CDF=~**: $c_k = \displaystyle \sum_{j=0}^{k} p_j$
4. **~={purple}Map=~**: $s_k = \text{round}\big((L-1)\cdot c_k\big)$
5. Replace every pixel of value $k$ with $s_k$. Several old levels may merge into one new level, so **add their counts** to get the new histogram.
	
	
> [!quote]+ ~={yellowlight}**Why the CDF?**=~
> Because the CDF always monotonically increases from:
> ```text
> 0 → ... → 1
> ```
> We then multiply it by:
> $$
> L-1
> $$
> (where $L$ is the number of intensity levels) to map it to a new intensity value.
> If we have 8 levels (0 to 7):
> $$
> L=8 \implies L-1=7
> $$
> 
> The mapping formula is:
> $$
> s_k = \text{round}((L-1) \cdot CDF(k))
> $$
> 
> Which becomes:
> $$
> s_k = \text{round}(7 \cdot CDF(k))
> $$
> 
	
> [!Example]- ~={yellowlight}Let's Apply the Example=~
> 
> We have:
> 
> | Intensity ($r_k$) | Count ($n_k$) |
> | ----------------: | ------------: |
> |                 0 |            70 |
> |                 1 |           100 |
> |                 2 |            40 |
> |                 3 |            60 |
> |                 4 |            10 |
> |                 5 |            70 |
> |                 6 |            10 |
> |                 7 |            40 |
> 
> Total number of pixels:
> $$
> n=400
> $$
> 
> ---
> 
> ##### Step 1 & 2 — PDF and CDF
> 
> First, calculate PDF ($n_k / n$), then cumulative sum for CDF:
> 
> ```text
> Intensity 0:
> PDF = 70/400 = 0.175
> CDF = 0.175
> 
> Intensity 1:
> PDF = 100/400 = 0.250
> CDF = 0.175 + 0.250 = 0.425
> 
> Intensity 2:
> PDF = 40/400 = 0.100
> CDF = 0.425 + 0.100 = 0.525
> ```
> 
> Continuing this gives:
> 
> | $r_k$ | CDF($r_k$) |
> | :---: | :--------: |
> |   0   |   0.175    |
> |   1   |   0.425    |
> |   2   |   0.525    |
> |   3   |   0.675    |
> |   4   |   0.700    |
> |   5   |   0.875    |
> |   6   |   0.900    |
> |   7   |   1.000    |
> 
> Notice how the CDF always steps upward, ending at 1.000.
> 
> ---
> 
> ##### Step 3 — Mapping
> 
> We apply:
> $$
> s_k = \text{round}(7 \cdot CDF(k))
> $$
> 
> For intensity 0:
> $$
> \text{round}(7 \times 0.175) = \text{round}(1.225) = 1
> $$
> ```text
> 0 → 1
> ```
> 
> For intensity 1:
> $$
> \text{round}(7 \times 0.425) = \text{round}(2.975) = 3
> $$
> ```text
> 1 → 3
> ```
> 
> For intensity 2:
> $$
> \text{round}(7 \times 0.525) = \text{round}(3.675) = 4
> $$
> ```text
> 2 → 4
> ```
> 
> Continuing this for all values:
> ```text
> Old → New
> 0 → 1
> 1 → 3
> 2 → 4
> 3 → 5
> 4 → 5
> 5 → 6
> 6 → 6
> 7 → 7
> ```
> 
> ---
> 
> ##### Crucial Point: Multiple values can map to the SAME new value
> 
> Notice that:
> ```text
> 3 → 5
> 4 → 5
> ```
> 
> This means:
> ```text
> old intensity 3 had 60 pixels
> old intensity 4 had 10 pixels
> ```
> 
> Both are now mapped to:
> ```text
> new intensity = 5
> ```
> 
> Therefore, the new count for intensity 5 is:
> $$
> new\_h(5) = 60 + 10 = 70
> $$
> 
> Similarly:
> ```text
> 5 → 6
> 6 → 6
> ```
> $$
> new\_h(6) = 70 + 10 = 80
> $$
> 
> **This is the exact reason why the resulting histogram is rarely perfectly flat.**
> 
	
> [!quote]+ ~={yellowlight}Why doesn't Equalization give a perfectly flat histogram?=~
> This is a very common exam/interview question.
> 
> Theoretically, in the **continuous** case:
> $$
> s=(L-1)\int_0^r p(w)dw
> $$
> This can lead to a perfectly uniform distribution.
> 
> But real digital images are:
> * Discrete
> * Have a limited number of pixels
> * Have a limited number of intensity levels (e.g., 256)
> * Require rounding
> * Multiple old intensities often merge into the same new intensity
> 
> Therefore, the output is only **approximately** uniform, not perfectly flat.

# Part 3: Quick comparison (exam cheat sheet)

|Technique|Formula|Best for|
|---|---|---|
|Negative|$s=L-1-r$|Light details on dark background|
|Log|$s=c\log(1+r)$|Very dark images, huge dynamic range|
|Gamma|$s=cr^\gamma$|$\gamma<1$ brighten, $\gamma>1$ darken|
|Piecewise linear|3 line segments|Custom contrast control|
|Contrast stretch|$\frac{x-x_{min}}{x_{max}-x_{min}}255$|Narrow histogram, simple fix|
|Hist. equalization|$s_k=\text{round}((L-1),\text{CDF}_k)$|Automatic contrast, uneven histograms|

**Key takeaways**

1. Point operations are just curves $s=T(r)$ applied per pixel, so LUTs make them fast.
2. Local operations use neighborhoods (filters), and global operations use the whole image (equalization, Fourier).
3. Contrast stretching **scales** the histogram, while equalization **reshapes** it.
4. Always normalize for gamma, and always use the real $x_{min}$ and $x_{max}$ for stretching.
5. In OpenCV, images are **BGR**, not RGB.
